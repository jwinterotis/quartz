# Andō Hiroshige (安藤 広重)

**Lived**: 1797-1858

## Biography
- Born in Edo to a family of fire wardens
- Became an orphan at age 12
- Studied with [[Utagawa Toyohiro]]
- Initially worked as a government official while pursuing art
- Later devoted himself entirely to art
- Died during a cholera epidemic in 1858

## Major Works
- **"Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō"** (東海道五十三次, 1833-1834)
  - Commercially successful landscape series
  - Innovative atmospheric effects
  - Multiple editions printed due to popularity
- **"One Hundred Famous Views of Edo"** (名所江戸百景, 1856-1858)
  - Vertical format innovations
  - Bold compositional techniques
  - Documented Edo before modernization
- **"Famous Views of the Sixty-odd Provinces"** (六十余州名所図会, 1853-1856)
  - Panoramic treatments of Japanese landscapes
  - Commercial success as collectible series

## Commercial Design Innovations
- **Atmospheric Effects**
  - Developed bokashi (gradation) printing techniques
  - Weather and seasonal phenomena representations
  - Time-of-day visual treatments
- **Series Design**
  - Consistent branding across multi-print series
  - Standardized formatting with variation
  - Created collectible commercial products
- **Compositional Techniques**
  - Foreground framing devices
  - Dramatic perspective
  - Simplified forms for commercial impact

## Commercial Context
- Published primarily by [[Hōeidō]] and [[Tsutaya]]
- Prints sold at affordable prices to middle-class consumers
- Travel scenes appealed to growing tourism market
- Images served as commercial souvenirs and mementos

## International Influence
- Collected by Western artists including Van Gogh and Whistler
- Influenced Impressionist and Post-Impressionist composition
- Landscape treatments impacted Western pictorial space
- Vertical format influenced poster design development

## Relevance to Research Question
Hiroshige's work exemplifies the commercial sophistication of Edo period design. His landscape series were commercial products designed for mass consumption, with standardized formats, consistent branding, and technical innovations specifically developed for commercial reproduction. The later influence of his compositions on Western art and design demonstrates how Japanese commercial visual strategies predated and subsequently influenced Western "modernist" approaches.
