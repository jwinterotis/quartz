# Katsushika Hokusai (葛飾 北斎)

**Lived**: 1760-1849

## Biography
- Born in Edo (Tokyo) in 1760
- Used over 30 different names throughout his career
- Worked into his 80s, famously said at 75 that he was "just beginning to understand how to draw"
- Considered himself in constant artistic development
- Died in 1849 at age 89

## Major Works
- **"Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji"** (富嶽三十六景, 1830-1832)
  - Includes "The Great Wave off Kanagawa"
  - Revolutionary use of Prussian blue (imported pigment)
- **"Hokusai Manga"** (北斎漫画, 1814-1878)
  - 15 volumes of sketches
  - Served as design reference for many other artists
  - Early example of instructional design guide
- **"One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji"** (富嶽百景, 1834)
  - Monochrome illustrations showing compositional mastery
- **"Hokusai Shashin Gafu"** (北斎写真画譜, 1814)
  - Design manual with drawing instructions

## Technical Innovations
- Pioneered integration of Western perspective techniques
- Developed complex color gradations (bokashi)
- Created systematic approach to depicting natural phenomena
- Established new compositional strategies (dramatic foreground elements, asymmetry)

## Commercial Impact
- Works widely distributed through publisher [[Eijudō]]
- Created designs for commercial application beyond fine art
- Established techniques later adopted by advertising
- Developed instructional methods for design education

## International Influence
- Profoundly influenced Western artists after Japan's opening
- Collected by Impressionists and Art Nouveau designers
- "The Great Wave" became one of the most recognized images worldwide
- Design principles incorporated into Western visual arts education

## Relevance to Research Question
Hokusai exemplifies how Edo period commercial design achieved sophisticated visual innovation outside Western contexts. His systematic approach to design, commercial distribution methods, and technical innovations all developed independently from Western design traditions. The later "discovery" and appropriation of his principles by Western designers in the late 19th century demonstrates how Japanese commercial design predated and influenced what would later be claimed as "modern" design innovations in the West.
