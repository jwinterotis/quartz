# Western Design Timeline

## Pre-Industrial Period
- **1450s**: Gutenberg [[Printing Press]] - movable type printing begins in Europe
- **1620s-1670s**: [[Other/Dutch Golden Age]] - Book illustration flourishes but with limited color reproduction
- **1650s**: Intaglio printing develops in Europe - sophisticated but labor-intensive
- **1660s**: [[Mezzotint Technique]] (tonal printing) develops but remains limited to fine art contexts
- **1702**: First daily newspaper in England ([[The Daily Courant]]) - limited to text and simple graphics

## Industrial Revolution and Beyond
- **1760s-1830s**: [[Industrial Revolution]] begins in England
- **1796**: Lithography invented by Alois Senefelder - still not widely used commercially until decades later
- **1814**: Steam-powered printing press (decades after Japan's commercial publishing boom)
- **1837**: Chromolithography invented - color printing finally becomes commercially viable in West
- **1850s**: [[Arts and Crafts]] begins as reaction against industrial production
- **1860s**: Western "discovery" of Japanese prints begins influencing European artists
- **1886**: Linotype machine invented - first major text composition advancement
- **1890s**: [[Art Nouveau]] emerges, heavily influenced by Japanese design aesthetics

## Key Comparative Points
- Japanese commercial color printing (1765) predates Western commercial color printing (1837) by over 70 years
- Edo publishing networks existed at mass scale without industrial power (hand-produced)
- Japanese visual branding systems for publishers developed before Western corporate identity concepts
- Western "modernist" design principles like asymmetry and flat color were standard in Edo commercial design

## Challenges to Western Design Origin Narrative
- Western design history often presents a linear progression from Gutenberg to Modernism
- This narrative ignores the sophisticated commercial design systems of Edo Japan
- Japan achieved mass visual culture without industrialization
- Western "modern" design principles emerged after exposure to Japanese commercial art
