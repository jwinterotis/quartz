# Dejima

Dejima (出島, "exit island") was a small artificial island in the bay of Nagasaki that served as the sole trading post for European (primarily Dutch) merchants during Japan's period of isolation.

## Historical Context
- Created in 1634, originally for Portuguese traders
- From 1641-1853, served as the only sanctioned point of direct trade and exchange between Japan and Europe
- Approximately 15,000 square meters in size (about the size of two football fields)

## Significance to Design Exchange
- **Technical Knowledge Transfer**: Dutch books on science, medicine, and art techniques entered Japan through Dejima
- **Western Visual Materials**: European prints, illustrations, and maps were studied by Japanese artists
- **Rangaku** ("Dutch Learning"): Japanese scholars studied Western knowledge through materials that came through Dejima
- **Perspective Techniques**: Western linear perspective entered Japanese art vocabulary through materials from Dejima

## Key Figures Associated with Dejima
- **[[Shiba Kōkan]]**: Studied Western art techniques through Dutch materials
- **[[Sugita Genpaku]]**: Medical scholar who accessed Western anatomical drawings
- **[[Hiraga Gennai]]**: Polymath who studied Western scientific illustrations
- **Philipp Franz von Siebold**: German physician who brought Western scientific illustrations to Japan

## Impact on Japanese Commercial Design
- Introduction of copper-plate etching technique
- Influence on representation of spatial depth
- Introduction of Western cartographic conventions
- Limited but significant exposure to Western typography

## Relationship to Research Question
Dejima represents the controlled interface between Western and Japanese visual cultures during the Edo period. Despite limited contact, Japanese designers selectively adapted Western techniques while maintaining their own commercial systems and aesthetic priorities. 

This selective adaptation—rather than wholesale adoption—demonstrates the sophistication of Japanese design judgment and the strength of existing commercial design practices, further supporting the argument that Edo commercial design was an independent, advanced system that challenges Western-centric design history narratives.
