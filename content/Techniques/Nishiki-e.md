# Nishiki-e (錦絵)

Nishiki-e, literally "brocade pictures," refers to full-color woodblock printing developed in Japan during the Edo period.

## Technical Development
- **First Appearance**: 1765, pioneered by [[Suzuki Harunobu]]
- **Technical Innovation**: Used multiple woodblocks (often 10-20) for a single print
- **Registration System**: Precise kentō (corner notches) allowed accurate color alignment
- **Scale**: Mass-produced for commercial distribution

## Process and Materials
- **Woodblocks**: Cherry wood (sakura) preferred for durability and fine detail
- **Paper**: Washi (handmade Japanese paper) with specific sizing for color absorption
- **Pigments**: Initially natural pigments, later some synthetic colors imported via [[Dejima]]
- **Production Team**:
  - **Eshi** (絵師): Designer who created the original drawing
  - **Horishi** (彫師): Carver who created the woodblocks
  - **Surishi** (刷師): Printer who applied colors and produced prints

## Commercial Significance
- **Breakthrough Product**: Created new market for affordable full-color art
- **Mass Production**: Popular designs often printed in runs of thousands
- **Marketing Innovation**: Created new visual possibilities for advertising
- **Cultural Impact**: Democratized access to sophisticated visual culture

## Comparative Context
- **Western Equivalents**: No comparable full-color printing technique existed in the West until chromolithography (1837), over 70 years later
- **Chinese Precedent**: Douban technique from Ming Dynasty influenced early development but was less commercially applied
- **Production Scale**: Achieved mass-production scale without industrial technology

## Influential Examples
- **[[Suzuki Harunobu]]'s Bijinga** (beautiful women): First commercial application
- **[[Katsushika Hokusai]]'s Landscapes**: Technically complex color gradations
- **[[Andō Hiroshige]]'s "53 Stations of the Tōkaidō"**: Commercial success with sophisticated color

## Evidence for Research Question
- Nishiki-e represented a technical achievement in color reproduction that predated Western capabilities by over seven decades
- The process demonstrated Japanese innovation in commercial production systems without industrial technology
- The aesthetic achievement influenced Western modern design principles when discovered in the 19th century
- This challenges the Western narrative that places technical innovation in visual reproduction primarily in the European industrial context
