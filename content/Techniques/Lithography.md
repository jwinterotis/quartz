# Lithography

Lithography is a printing process invented in 1796 by German author and actor Alois Senefelder, based on the chemical repulsion of oil and water.

## Technical Development
- **Invented**: 1796 by Alois Senefelder in Bavaria
- **Basic Principle**: Drawing with oil-based material on limestone; treating with acid to fix image
- **1837**: Chromolithography (color lithography) developed by Godefroy Engelmann
- **1870s**: Widespread commercial adoption for advertising and packaging
- **1890s-1900s**: Art lithography flourishes with artists like Toulouse-Lautrec

## Technical Process
- **Stone Preparation**: Polishing limestone surface
- **Image Creation**: Drawing with greasy crayon or ink (positive areas)
- **Chemical Processing**: Treating stone with gum arabic solution and acid
- **Printing**: Wetting stone (water adheres to non-image areas), inking (oil-based ink adheres to image areas), pressing paper against stone

## Comparison to Japanese Woodblock Printing
- **[[Nishiki-e]] Precedence**: Japanese multi-color woodblock printing developed in 1765, predating chromolithography by 72 years
- **Color Approaches**: Chromolithography required separate stone for each color, similar to separate woodblocks in ukiyo-e
- **Registration Systems**: Both required precise registration for color alignment
- **Commercial Scale**: Japanese woodblock achieved commercial scale earlier without industrial power
- **Aesthetic Quality**: Both achieved sophisticated color reproduction but with different visual characteristics

## Commercial Application
- **Western Context**: Became primary commercial printing method in late 19th century Europe
- **Advertising Development**: Enabled colorful posters and advertisements
- **Product Packaging**: Used for labels, wrappers, and packages
- **Publication Revolution**: Allowed illustrated periodicals and color book printing

## Cultural Impact
- **Democratization of Images**: Made color reproduction more widely available
- **Advertising Development**: Facilitated visual marketing revolution
- **Visual Culture**: Transformed Western streetscapes with colorful posters
- **Artistic Medium**: Adopted by fine artists alongside commercial use

## Relevance to Research Question
Lithography represents a key Western printing development, yet its color application (chromolithography) emerged over 70 years after Japanese [[Nishiki-e]] color printing. This chronology directly challenges Western-centric design narratives that place technical innovation primarily in European contexts. While lithography eventually became the dominant Western commercial printing technique, Japanese woodblock printing had already achieved sophisticated mass color reproduction, commercial scale, and visual marketing applications long before Western lithographic processes could match these capabilities. This timing disparity provides clear evidence that Edo commercial design achieved technical and commercial innovations that predated comparable Western developments by many decades.
