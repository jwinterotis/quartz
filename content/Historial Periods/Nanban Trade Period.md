# Nanban Trade Period (1543-1639)

The Nanban ("Southern Barbarian") Trade Period refers to the early interactions between Japan and European powers, particularly Portugal and Spain, before Japan's period of isolation.

## Historical Timeline
- **1543**: Portuguese arrive in Japan (Tanegashima)
- **1549**: Francis Xavier begins Jesuit mission in Japan
- **1570s-1590s**: Height of Nanban trade
- **1600**: William Adams (English) arrives in Japan on Dutch ship
- **1609**: Dutch trading post established at Hirado
- **1612-1620**: Anti-Christian edicts issued
- **1637-38**: Shimabara Rebellion
- **1639**: Portuguese expelled; Japan enters sakoku (closed country) period

## Commercial and Cultural Exchange
- **Trade Goods**:
  - Portuguese imported Chinese silk, European clocks, glasses, firearms
  - Japan exported silver, copper, lacquerware, folding screens
- **Religious Exchange**:
  - Jesuit missionaries introduced Western religious imagery
  - Creation of "Nanban art" combining Japanese and Western elements
- **Knowledge Transfer**:
  - Western astronomical and geographical knowledge
  - Medical information and techniques
  - Printing technology (movable type briefly used)

## Visual Culture Impact
- **Nanban Screens** (南蛮屏風):
  - Folding screens depicting Portuguese ships, traders, and missionaries
  - Created by Japanese artists of Kano school
  - Combined Japanese artistic techniques with Western subject matter
- **Nanban Lacquerware**:
  - Japanese lacquer techniques applied to Western-style objects
  - Created specifically for export market
  - Combined Japanese craftsmanship with European forms
- **Early Western-style Painting**:
  - Japanese artists trained by Jesuits in European techniques
  - Oil painting introduced to Japan
  - Western perspective techniques first encountered

## Printing and Design Significance
- **Kirishitan-ban** (Christian publications):
  - Early printing using movable type in Japan
  - Western-style book formats introduced
  - Combination of Western and Japanese typography
- **Western-style Maps**:
  - Introduction of European cartographic techniques
  - Japanese adaptations of Western world maps
  - New visual approach to geographical information

## Key Figures
- **Alessandro Valignano** (1539-1606): Jesuit missionary who established printing presses in Japan
- **Hasekura Tsunenaga** (1571-1622): Japanese diplomat who visited Europe
- **Shiba Kōkan** (1747-1818): Later artist who studied Western techniques introduced during this period

## Legacy and Relevance to Research Question
Although the Nanban period was brief, it provided Japan's first direct exposure to Western visual culture and printing techniques. These early exchanges influenced later Edo period artists, particularly those interested in Western-style realism, perspective, and printing technologies. The Japanese response to these European influences demonstrates selective adaptation rather than wholesale adoption, showing how Japanese visual culture maintained its distinct character while incorporating useful external elements.

This period is significant to your research question because it shows that early Western-Japanese encounters did not fundamentally alter Japanese commercial design trajectories. Instead, Japanese artists and craftspeople selectively adopted specific technical elements while maintaining their own aesthetic and commercial frameworks. This selective adaptation demonstrates the sophistication and confidence of Japanese visual culture even when exposed to Western alternatives.
